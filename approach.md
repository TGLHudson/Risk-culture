Hudson Risk Culture (HRC) starts from one position: the culture an organisation needs is set by the risks it runs, not by a ranking of maturity.

By risk culture we mean the shared practices through which an organisation recognises, discusses and manages the risks it runs; safety culture is one part of it. Most of our published work to date is in high-hazard operations, with some on road traffic risk.

## Fit, not maturity

Maturity models, the safety culture ladder among them, are usually read as scales to climb, and our own earlier work used the ladder that way. Taken as a climb, culture becomes a score to improve in the abstract. We ask a different question: given the hazards, the operations and the uncertainty this organisation faces, what culture does it need, and does the culture it has fit? On this view, the ladder is a map of fit, not a scoreboard.

## Where the ladder comes from

Ron Westrum described three organisational cultures: pathological, bureaucratic and generative. Professor P. T. W. Hudson extended them into the safety culture ladder; the five-stage version followed in 2000, with Dianne Parker, Matthew Lawrie and colleagues. Westrum himself has not accepted the two added stages. Professor Hudson is T. G. L. Hudson's father, and we state that relationship openly. T. G. L. Hudson leads Hudson Risk Culture; his book *The Culture Ladder*, due from CRC Press in February 2027, builds on Professor Hudson's ladder and reframes it around fit to risk. ## Working against the risk

We are not working for management; we are working against the risk. An engagement succeeds when management and workforce agree on how the risks being run are managed. The work is pro-worker and pro-union: the people closest to the hazard carry the risk, and they are part of the agreement, not its subject.
